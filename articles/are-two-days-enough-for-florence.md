Florence's historic centre is about a kilometre and a half across. You can walk from the station to Ponte Vecchio in fifteen minutes and from the Duomo to Santa Croce in ten. There is no metro because nothing is far enough away to need one.

That geography is the whole answer. Two days of walking really does cover the city, and you lose almost nothing to getting around.

What Florence has instead of distance is depth: seventy-odd museums, most of them worth an afternoon. Two days is enough for exactly one of them per day, and the trip goes well or badly depending on whether you accept that.

<p><strong>One booked museum per day.</strong> Under that limit, two days in Florence feels generous. Over it, the second museum gets rushed and the third is barely remembered. It is the same two days either way.</p>

## Why the size of the city decides this

In Rome or Paris a short visit is a logistics problem: an hour a day disappears into transport. Here it does not. You will walk everywhere, and the walking is the good part, because the city was built at that speed and reveals itself accordingly.

So the constraint is not time or distance. It is attention. Four galleries in two days produces a blur; one gallery a day produces a trip you can still describe a year later.

## Day one

Start at the Duomo around eight, before the square fills. The exterior is the point: the marble, the scale, the Baptistery doors. That costs nothing and takes as long as you want.

Then one climb, if you want one. The dome is 463 steps in a narrow spiral with no way to turn back, and you pass Vasari's frescoes at close range on the way. Giotto's campanile next door is shorter, less enclosed, and puts the dome in your photograph rather than under your feet.

Walk down to Piazza della Signoria and stand in the Loggia dei Lanzi, which is an open air sculpture gallery and free. Cross Ponte Vecchio, then eat lunch on the far side rather than near the bridge.

In the afternoon, the Bargello. An hour, rarely a queue, and it holds Donatello's bronze David along with the two competition panels by Ghiberti and Brunelleschi. If you prefer painting, the Brancacci Chapel across the river is the equivalent choice.

In the evening, stay in the Oltrarno. Santo Spirito is a working square with a plain unfinished church face and bars around the edge, and it is where the city stops performing.

## Day two

Morning: the Accademia, with a booked slot. Forty five minutes, built around one object. The corridor of unfinished Prisoners on the way to David is the part people do not expect.

Lunch at Sant'Ambrogio or the Mercato Centrale, both working markets.

Afternoon: get out of the flat part of the city. Erta Canina climbs out of San Niccolo through olive terraces, and the Viale dei Colli runs along the hillside from Porta Romana. Both come out near San Miniato al Monte and the Giardino delle Rose, both are free, and both give you the view.

If it is hot, swap the climb for two hours on the Arno. The boats leave from the beach under Torre di San Niccolo, twelve minutes on foot east of Ponte Vecchio, and the river is the coolest place in Florence in August.

Evening: Ponte Santa Trinita at dusk, for Ponte Vecchio lit up over the water, then dinner wherever you have ended up.

## Book these three things

The Accademia and the Uffizi both use timed entry, and the dome climb sells out several days ahead in summer.

That is the entire list. Everything else in this article can be decided on the morning, which is part of why a short visit works here.

## If one of your days is a Monday

Many Italian state museums close on Mondays, and several Florentine museums also close on particular Sundays of the month. Check the specific opening days for whatever you have chosen before you build a day around it.

If Monday is one of your two days, put the outdoor half there. The Duomo square, San Miniato, the Oltrarno, the markets and the river all stay open.
