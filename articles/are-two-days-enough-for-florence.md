This question comes up constantly on travel forums, and the answers split cleanly in two. People who spent two days and enjoyed it say the city is small and walkable. People who spent two days and felt cheated almost always describe the same itinerary: the Uffizi, the Accademia, the Pitti Palace and the dome, packed into forty eight hours, with the queues in between.

Both groups are right about the facts and reached different conclusions because they did different trips.

## The short answer

Two days is enough for Florence if you accept one hard limit: **one booked museum per day, maximum.** Under that rule, two days is genuinely satisfying. Break it and you will spend your visit in lines and remember very little.

## Why two days works here and not in Rome

Florence's historic centre is roughly a kilometre and a half across. You can walk from the station to Ponte Vecchio in fifteen minutes, and from the Duomo to Santa Croce in ten. There is no metro because there is nothing far enough away to need one.

That geography is the entire reason a short visit works. In Rome you would lose an hour a day just moving between things. Here you lose almost nothing, so two days of walking really does cover the city.

What Florence has instead is depth. Seventy-odd museums, and the temptation to treat the visit as a checklist. That is the trap, and it is the only one.

## Day one

Start at the Duomo at eight, before the square fills. The outside is the point: the marble, the scale, the Baptistery doors. That costs nothing and takes as long as you like.

Then one thing with a ticket. Either the dome climb, if you booked it and can face 463 steps in a spiral, or Giotto's campanile next door, which is shorter and puts the dome in your photograph.

Walk to Piazza della Signoria and the Loggia dei Lanzi, which is an open air sculpture gallery you can stand in for free. Cross Ponte Vecchio without stopping long, then eat lunch in the Oltrarno rather than near the bridge, where prices roughly double for worse food.

Afternoon: the Bargello, an hour, rarely a queue, and it holds Donatello's David and the panels that started the Renaissance. Or the Brancacci Chapel if you prefer painting.

Evening: cross back into Santo Spirito and stay there. This is where Florence stops performing.

## Day two

Morning: the Accademia, booked. Forty five minutes, built around one object, and the least painful famous museum in Italy.

Then Sant'Ambrogio market or the Mercato Centrale for lunch, both of them working markets rather than attractions.

Afternoon: leave the flat part of the city. Walk up Erta Canina from Porta San Miniato, or take the Viale dei Colli from Porta Romana. Both come out near San Miniato al Monte and the Giardino delle Rose, both are free, and both give you the view without the crowd at Piazzale Michelangelo.

If it is hot, swap the walk for two hours on the Arno. The rafting boats leave from the beach under Torre di San Niccolo, twelve minutes on foot east of Ponte Vecchio, and the river is the coolest place in the city in August.

Evening: Ponte Santa Trinita at dusk for the view of Ponte Vecchio lit up, then dinner wherever you ended up.

## The three things that eat the most time

**Unbooked queues.** Turning up at the Uffizi or the Accademia without a slot can cost an hour or more in season. This is entirely avoidable and it is the single biggest waste of a two day trip.

**A day trip.** Siena and Pisa are frequently recommended and both are a mistake on a two day stay. The round trip eats half a day and you end up having seen neither Florence nor the other place properly.

**Lunch in the wrong square.** A sit down lunch on a main piazza can take ninety minutes and cost forty euro. A stand up plate of pasta or a lampredotto roll takes fifteen minutes and costs under ten, and it is usually better.

## What to book before you arrive

The Accademia and the Uffizi, both timed entry. The dome climb, which sells out several days ahead in summer. That is the whole list.

Everything else in this article can be decided on the morning, which is part of why two days works.

## If your two days include a Monday

This catches people out. Many state museums in Italy close on Mondays, and several Florentine museums close on the first Sunday of the month or rotate their closures.

Check the specific opening days for whatever you have chosen before you build the day around it, and if Monday is one of your two days, put the churches, the markets, the hill walks and the river on that day rather than the galleries. The Duomo square, San Miniato, the Oltrarno and the Arno do not close.
