Florence has a crowding problem that is really a geography problem. The famous sights sit inside a square roughly a kilometre and a half across, and the great majority of visitors never leave it. Which means the pressure is enormous in that square and close to nonexistent two streets outside it.

This is good news if you know where the edges are. Nothing below is remote or difficult. Most of it is inside the old city walls, and the furthest thing on the list is twenty five minutes on a bus.

## Walk the Viale dei Colli end to end

When Florence was briefly the capital of Italy in the 1860s, Giuseppe Poggi laid out a boulevard along the hills of the south bank. It was built to be walked, and almost nobody walks the whole thing.

<div><dt>Start</dt><dd><a href="https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Porta%20Romana%2C%20Firenze" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Porta Romana</a>, fifteen minutes on foot from Ponte Vecchio up Via Romana</dd></div>
<div><dt>Finish</dt><dd><a href="https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Piazza%20Ferrucci%2C%20Firenze" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Piazza Ferrucci</a>, back down on the river</dd></div>
<div><dt>Time</dt><dd>About an hour, mostly downhill in that direction</dd></div>
</dl>
</div>

Piazzale Michelangelo sits in the middle of this walk, and it is the least interesting part of it. The two ends are shaded, quiet and almost empty even in August, with garden walls on one side and the city appearing and disappearing on the other.

Bus 12 covers the same route from Porta Romana if you would rather ride up and walk down. Going the other way, from Piazza Ferrucci, means climbing the whole thing, which is fine in April and a bad idea in July.

## Go up Erta Canina instead

<div class="venue">
<h3>Erta Canina</h3>
<dl class="venue-meta">
<div><dt>Start</dt><dd><a href="https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Porta%20San%20Miniato%2C%20Firenze" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Porta San Miniato</a>, twelve minutes east of Ponte Vecchio along the south bank</dd></div>
<div><dt>Time</dt><dd>Twenty minutes up, longer if you stop</dd></div>
<div><dt>Note</dt><dd>Steep and uneven, so shoes rather than sandals</dd></div>
</dl>
</div>

A narrow paved lane between high garden walls that leaves the city almost at once. Ten minutes up and you are among olive terraces, hearing birds instead of scooters, still technically inside the walls.

Partway up there is a gap in the stonework that frames the dome and the campanile exactly. Locals call it lo scorcio, the glimpse. There is no railing, no viewing platform and no queue, and it is a better photograph than the one everybody takes from the Piazzale.

From the top you are a few minutes from the Giardino delle Rose and from San Miniato al Monte, which means this connects to the Viale dei Colli walk if you want to make an afternoon of it.

## Fiesole, but walk back down

Fiesole is on every list, usually as a bus ride up and a bus ride back. The bus ride up is the boring half.

<div class="venue">
<h3>Fiesole on foot</h3>
<dl class="venue-meta">
<div><dt>Getting there</dt><dd>Bus 7 from <a href="https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Piazza%20San%20Marco%2C%20Firenze" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Piazza San Marco</a>, around twenty five minutes</dd></div>
<div><dt>Coming back</dt><dd>On foot down Via Vecchia Fiesolana, roughly an hour and a half</dd></div>
<div><dt>Best time</dt><dd>Late afternoon, so the descent is in soft light</dd></div>
</dl>
</div>

Take the bus up, look at the Roman theatre and the Etruscan walls, eat something in the square, then walk back down through San Domenico. The old road drops through cypress and villa gardens, past walls with olive trees behind them, with Florence spread out below and getting slowly closer. It is one of the great walks in Tuscany and it starts twenty five minutes from the Duomo.

Do it downhill. People who try it uphill in summer regret it.

## The collector's house on the hill

<div class="venue">
<h3>Museo Stibbert</h3>
<dl class="venue-meta">
<div><dt>Where</dt><dd><a href="https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Museo%20Stibbert%2C%20Via%20Federico%20Stibbert%2C%20Firenze" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Via Federico Stibbert 26</a>, on the Montughi hill</dd></div>
<div><dt>Getting there</dt><dd>Bus 4 from Santa Maria Novella station, about fifteen minutes</dd></div>
<div><dt>Time</dt><dd>An hour and a half, plus the park</dd></div>
</dl>
</div>

Frederick Stibbert was born in Florence to a British family, inherited a fortune and spent it on roughly fifty thousand objects. Arms and armour, European, Islamic and Japanese, plus costume, tapestries and furniture. When he died in 1906 he left the lot to the city, and it has been kept the way he arranged it rather than rearranged into something modern.

The room people remember is the Sala della Cavalcata: a procession of life sized figures in real armour, mounted, European and Ottoman, drawn up in formation down one long hall. There are samurai suits from the 1600s and a Bronzino self portrait too.

The park outside is free, laid out by the same Poggi who built the Viale dei Colli, and contains a lake, grottoes and a small Egyptian temple. It is usually empty.

## The park nobody sends you to

<div class="venue">
<h3>Parco di Villa il Ventaglio</h3>
<dl class="venue-meta">
<div><dt>Where</dt><dd><a href="https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Parco%20di%20Villa%20il%20Ventaglio%2C%20Firenze" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Via Aldini 12</a>, northeast of the centre</dd></div>
<div><dt>Getting there</dt><dd>Bus 10 from Piazza San Marco, or thirty five minutes on foot from Piazza Beccaria</dd></div>
<div><dt>Note</dt><dd>Seasonal hours and the gates do close</dd></div>
</dl>
</div>

A hillside park with a lake, an island, a neoclassical villa and long meadows that open onto the city. Poggi again, planting lime and horse chestnut across what had been olive groves so that the view keeps appearing between the trees.

The upper slopes give one of the best free views in Florence, and the difference from Piazzale Michelangelo is that you will often be the only person having it.

## Get on the river

The Arno runs through the middle of Florence and hardly anybody is on it. From the water the city looks like a different place, and the crowd on Ponte Vecchio is standing directly above you without a clue.

<div><dt>Getting there</dt><dd>Twelve minutes on foot east of Ponte Vecchio along the south bank</dd></div>

Rafting means everyone in one boat with a guide who paddles, so nobody needs any skill, and it takes children from about four. Kayaking is one small inflatable each and suits people who would rather do the work themselves. The stretch through the centre is flat with no rapids either way, and you pass directly under the arches of Ponte Vecchio.

Departure times shift with the season.

## A cinema inside a Renaissance palazzo

<div class="venue">
<h3>Giunti Odeon</h3>
<dl class="venue-meta">
<div><dt>Where</dt><dd><a href="https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Giunti%20Odeon%2C%20Piazza%20degli%20Strozzi%2C%20Firenze" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Piazza degli Strozzi</a></dd></div>
<div><dt>Getting there</dt><dd>Five minutes on foot from Piazza della Repubblica or the Duomo</dd></div>
<div><dt>Best for</dt><dd>Rain, heat, or an hour with nothing to do</dd></div>
</dl>
</div>

The Odeon opened as a cinema in 1922 inside Palazzo dello Strozzino, under a dome of coloured glass with gilded stucco and three carved Muses. It ran for nearly a century. In November 2023 it reopened as a bookshop and cinema together, after the regular audience objected to a plan that would have dropped the films.

What you get is around twenty five thousand books including English originals, a cafe with a wine window, and the old hall still screening films in their original language. During the day the upper gallery is open with seating and a screen and nobody rushes you out. This is the single best answer in central Florence to a wet afternoon, and it is on the street everybody is already walking down.

## Spend one day with no plan at all

The most underrated thing you can do here costs nothing and requires you to put the phone away.

Pick a direction, ideally south across the river or north past Sant'Ambrogio, and walk without a map, without a list and without checking whether the place you just passed has good reviews. Florence is small enough that you cannot properly get lost and dense enough that you will find something every few minutes: a courtyard with a well in it, a workshop with the door open, a church nobody has written about.

This is a real recommendation rather than a nice sentiment. The city was built at walking speed and it rewards being read that way. Everything on the list above is worth doing, but the day people describe afterwards is usually the one with nothing scheduled in it.
