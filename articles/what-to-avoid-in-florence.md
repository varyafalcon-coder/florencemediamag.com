Florence is an easy city to enjoy and a slightly tricky one to eat in. Almost nothing here will cheat you outright. What happens instead is quieter: you pay more and wait longer for a weaker version of something the city does extremely well two streets away.

Knowing where the line falls is most of the work.

## The three street rule

The expensive, ordinary version of Florence sits directly around the famous sights. Walk three streets away from the Duomo, Ponte Vecchio or Piazza della Signoria, in any direction, and prices drop, the menus get shorter, and the food gets better.

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>Three streets, in any direction.</strong> A restaurant with a view of a monument is paying for that view and passing the cost to you. The same meal three minutes' walk away is often half the price and cooked to order. This applies to food, coffee, wine and souvenirs.</p>
</div>

It works because the economics are simple. A place on a main square never needs a customer to come back, because a new one arrives every four minutes. A place on a side street lives on regulars, and regulars notice.

Once you know the rule you stop needing a list. You can walk into almost anything three streets off a piazza and do well.

## Restaurants in the main squares

The most expensive habit in Florence is sitting down for lunch on Piazza della Signoria, Piazza del Duomo or the streets immediately around Ponte Vecchio. Forty euro, ninety minutes, and food that was assembled rather than cooked.

What to look for instead is easy to spot from the doorway. A short menu, in Italian first, with a handwritten section that changes. A room where people are eating at Italian hours, which for dinner means after eight. Prices that make sense for what is listed.

One dish deserves its own note. The bistecca alla fiorentina is priced by weight, usually per hundred grams, and a real one weighs around a kilogram and feeds two or three people. Check the per unit price and the raw weight before it goes on the grill, so the number at the end is the one you expected.

## The queue for a sandwich

A couple of panini shops in the centre now have lines down the street for most of the day.

The sandwiches are genuinely good. The queue is the problem, not the food. Within three streets of each of them there are places doing the same thing, at the same quality, with nobody waiting. If you have two days in Florence, forty minutes is a real slice of the trip.

A queue in a city this dense tells you something about how well a place is known, not about how well it cooks.

## Gelato that will not melt

Real gelato is churned to be eaten within a day. It sits flat in covered metal tins, and it slumps.

The mounded, glossy, brightly coloured version has been stabilised to hold those peaks in a warm shop. Four checks take about three seconds at the door: flat in covered tins, grey banana, dull khaki pistachio, nothing bright blue.

We wrote the whole method out, with six places in Florence that pass, in [how to tell real gelato from the tourist stuff](/articles/real-gelato-in-florence/).

## Leather that was made elsewhere

Florence has a real leather tradition, and it is still working. There are workshops across the Oltrarno, and the Scuola del Cuoio behind Santa Croce lets you watch the cutting and stitching happen in the room.

The stalls around San Lorenzo are a different trade. Quality ranges from decent to poor, most of it was made somewhere else, and the first price assumes you will not ask again.

If you want a souvenir, buy wherever you like and enjoy it. If the leather itself matters, buy where the work is visible on the premises, and expect to pay what that costs. The same distinction applies to marbled paper, which is a genuine Florentine craft with a mass produced version sitting next to it on the shelf.
