Florence has surprisingly few actual scams. What it has instead is a dense concentration of ways to spend more money and more time than necessary on a worse version of what you came for. The good version is usually a few streets away and costs less.

The traps cluster in a small area: the streets between the Duomo, Ponte Vecchio and Piazza della Signoria. Understanding that geography is most of the defence.

## The tell that works everywhere

Anything designed for people who will never come back looks the same the world over.

A menu with photographs on it. A person standing outside inviting you in. A menu translated into five languages, laminated, with a section called "tourist menu". A shop selling the same three products as the four shops beside it.

The opposite signals are just as reliable. A short menu written in Italian first. A handwritten section that changes. A room where people are eating at times Italians eat, which for dinner means after eight.

## Restaurants with photographs on the menu

The single most expensive mistake in Florence is a sit down lunch on a main square. Forty euro, ninety minutes, and food that arrived reheated. It is not fraud, it is simply what a location with unlimited passing trade produces.

There is also a specific version worth knowing about: the bistecca alla fiorentina. It is priced by weight, usually per hundred grams, and a proper steak is about a kilogram and is meant for two or three people. Read the per unit price, check the weight before it is cooked, and understand that a genuine one will not be cheap. A cheap fiorentina is not a bargain, it is a different piece of meat.

The fix is not a secret list. Walk three streets off the piazza, in any direction, and look for the signals above.

## The queue for a sandwich

At some point the internet decided that one particular panino shop was the thing to do in Florence, and now there is a queue down the street for most of the day.

The sandwiches are good. They are not forty minutes good, and there are several places within three streets doing exactly the same thing with no line at all. If you are here for two days, that queue is a meaningful fraction of your trip.

The same applies to any food place with a line visible from the next street. In a city this dense, a queue is information about marketing rather than about quality.

## Gelato piled into mountains

Real gelato sits flat in covered metal tins. It is churned to be eaten within a day and it slumps.

The mounded, glossy, brightly coloured stuff piled into peaks has been stabilised to hold that shape, which is the opposite of the point. Grey banana, dull khaki pistachio, nothing bright blue. Those four checks take three seconds at the door and they work anywhere in Italy.

We wrote the whole method out in [how to tell real gelato from the tourist stuff](/articles/real-gelato-in-florence/), including six places in Florence that pass.

## Sunset at Piazzale Michelangelo

Not a trap exactly, but the most reliable way to have a worse evening than the one available.

The view is genuinely one of the best in Italy. The problem is that everybody has been told the same thing, so at sunset you are in a car park with several hundred other people, holding a phone above someone's head.

San Miniato al Monte is five minutes further uphill and almost empty, with the same view and Gregorian chant in the evening. The Giardino delle Rose is just below the Piazzale, free, and looks out through roses. And Ponte Santa Trinita, down at river level, gives you the shot of Ponte Vecchio lit up that most people came for anyway.

Go to the Piazzale once, in the morning, when it is quiet and the light is on the city rather than behind it.

## Leather that was not made here

Florence has a real leather tradition and it still exists, in workshops in the Oltrarno and at the Scuola del Cuoio behind Santa Croce, where you can watch the work happening.

The stalls around San Lorenzo are a different business. Quality varies from decent to poor, very little of it is made in Florence, and the price you are first quoted assumes you will not ask twice.

If leather is a souvenir, buy it wherever you like and enjoy it. If it matters, buy it somewhere the work is visible on the premises, and expect to pay accordingly.

The same logic covers marbled paper, which is a genuine Florentine craft with a genuine tourist imitation sitting next to it.

## Things that are fine and get a bad name

Not everything popular is a trap, and a few things get unfairly warned about.

**The coperto** is a legal per person cover charge, normally one to three euro, and it must be printed on the menu. It is not a scam and it is not a tip. Service is frequently included too. Rounding up is plenty; Italians do not tip the way Americans do.

**Tap water** is safe everywhere in Florence, including the public fountains, several of which run cold and free all summer.

**The Duomo square in the morning.** Being the most famous thing in the city does not make it overrated. At eight in the morning it is nearly empty and it is the best thing here.

**Ponte Vecchio** is worth crossing. It is only a trap if you plan an hour on it.

And the thing genuinely worth avoiding has nothing to do with money: booking three museums into two days. It costs more of your trip than every overpriced lunch in this article put together.
