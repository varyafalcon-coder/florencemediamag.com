Six things, and the order matters more than the length of the list. Two of them need booking. The rest you can decide on the morning.

## The one that is genuinely unmissable

The Duomo complex, and the outside of it before anything else. Santa Maria del Fiore, Giotto's campanile and the Baptistery are on the same small square, all faced in green, white and pink marble, and nothing in a photograph prepares you for the scale.

Inside, the cathedral is comparatively plain, and the queue is long. The exterior costs nothing and takes as long as you want. Go early, around eight, when the square is nearly empty and the light hits the west front.

If you climb something, the choice matters. The dome is 463 steps in a narrow spiral with no turning back, and the real reward is passing Vasari's frescoes at close range. Giotto's campanile is a shorter climb, less claustrophobic, and it puts the dome itself in your photograph rather than under your feet.

## David, and why it survives the hype

The Accademia is a short visit built around one object, which is exactly what you want when time is limited. David is seventeen feet of marble at the end of a corridor, and the thing photographs never convey is that Michelangelo carved it at twenty six from a block another sculptor had abandoned as ruined.

The corridor leading up to it holds the unfinished Prisoners, half emerged from their stone. Some people find those more affecting than David himself.

Forty five minutes covers it. Book a time slot and it is one of the least painful famous things in Italy.

## One Renaissance room, not five

Florence has enough art to fill a month. On a short visit, one gallery with proper attention beats four at speed.

A medieval fortress and prison turned sculpture museum, holding Donatello's bronze David, work by Michelangelo and Cellini, and the two competition panels by Ghiberti and Brunelleschi that effectively started the Renaissance. It is a fraction of the Uffizi's crowd for a comparable density of important objects.

The alternative, if you prefer painting, is the Brancacci Chapel across the river, where Masaccio worked out how to paint space and every painter in Florence came to study afterwards. Small, quiet, ticketed, and one of the genuinely important rooms in European art.

Either will give you more in an hour than a long gallery on tired legs. If galleries are the reason you came, go to the Uffizi instead, with a booking and a plan.

## Where to be at sunset

Piazzale Michelangelo has the postcard view and, at sunset, most of the people who came for it. Three alternatives sit within a few minutes of it.

An eleventh century church with a marble facade, a crypt, and monks who still sing vespers in the evening. Five minutes above the Piazzale and far quieter.

The Giardino delle Rose sits just below, free, with the same panorama through roses and Folon sculptures, and closes at dusk. Down at river level, Ponte Santa Trinita gives you Ponte Vecchio lit gold over the water.

## The Oltrarno after dark

Cross the river in the evening and Florence stops being a museum. Santo Spirito is a working square with a bar-lined edge and a plain unfinished church face, full of Florentines rather than tour groups. The streets around it, Via Maggio, Via dello Sprone, Borgo San Frediano, still hold the workshops the city was built on: gilders, restorers, leather.

This costs nothing and needs no booking, and it is the part people describe when they get home.

## One meal that is not a tourist menu

Eating badly in Florence takes effort to avoid, because the centre is dense with places serving reheated food to people who will never come back. The fix is a rule rather than a list: eat where the menu is short, in Italian first, and changes.

A stand-up plate of pasta, a lampredotto roll from a cart, a bowl of ribollita in a trattoria with paper tablecloths. Under fifteen euro, and better than the forty euro version on the main square.

## What you can leave out

Ponte Vecchio works better as something you cross than somewhere you stand. The view of it from Ponte Santa Trinita is the better one.

The long queue for a famous sandwich, when three streets away the same thing is being made with nobody waiting.

And the Uffizi, on a two day visit, unless art is the reason you came. One museum seen properly is the better trade.
